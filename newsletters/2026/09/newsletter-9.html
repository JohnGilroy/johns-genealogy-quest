Military Service in the Family

What surviving service records can tell us about the people behind the names

Over the past few weeks I have been working through the military records in the family history collection.

Many of these records had accumulated over the years as groups of individual downloaded images: attestation papers, medical forms, service statements, pension records, casualty sheets and discharge documents. The immediate task has been preservation and organisation. For each man, the surviving images have now been gathered into a single evidence package, with a short synopsis, chronology and the original documents in their proper order.

That sounds like housekeeping, and in one sense it is. But the work has also been a reminder of how much these records can tell us.

A service record may give an age, birthplace, occupation, physical description, next of kin, medical history, conduct assessment, overseas postings, discharge reason and sometimes even a revealing comment that would never appear in a civil register or census entry.

This month's newsletter is therefore not really about military history. It is about family history: what these records reveal about the people behind the names.

James Stretton - a boy soldier and Waterloo

James Stretton, my fourth great-grandfather, was born in Birmingham and worked as a labourer before joining the 73rd Regiment of Foot at Nottingham on 11 August 1809.

He was only fifteen.

His earliest service was therefore recorded as under age. Later he served as both drummer and private. What makes his papers especially striking is the calculation made when he was discharged in 1834. His adult reckonable service was supplemented by an explicit allowance of two additional years for Waterloo, producing a final total of 24 years and 33 days.

Those two years were not extra calendar years actually served. They were a pensionable-service credit recognising his participation in the Waterloo campaign.

The papers also bring him sharply into view as a person. He was discharged because of chronic ulceration of the right leg and thigh. His conduct was recorded as good, although with the candid qualification that he was "rather addicted to drink", for which he had been punished.

A database entry might simply say that James served in the 73rd Foot. The original papers tell us that he joined as a boy, lived through the Waterloo period, suffered long-term ill health and left behind a very human, very unvarnished assessment of his character.

Peter Penman - from Cockpen to Gibraltar

Peter Penman, my fourth great-uncle, was a miner from Cockpen when he attested for the 42nd Regiment of Foot at Edinburgh on 8 December 1817, aged 19.

His papers record service at Gibraltar from November 1826 until May 1832. By the time his regiment considered his discharge in 1836, he had accumulated 18 years and 280 days of reckonable service.

The most revealing part of Peter's file is medical. His papers record severe varicose veins of the legs and later chronic pulmonary disease. After treatment at the General Hospital, Chatham, he was considered permanently unfit for further military duty. Importantly, the board found that his disability had arisen in service and was not caused by neglect, design, vice or intemperance.

His discharge description also freezes a moment in his life. At 38 he was recorded as five feet ten inches tall, with brown hair, hazel eyes and a swarthy complexion. His occupation was still miner.

For ancestors who lived before photography became common, this kind of document may be the closest we ever get to seeing them.

Joseph Hinshelwood - two very different military careers

Joseph Hinshelwood was the half-brother of my great-grandfather William Hinshelwood. His case is one of the most unusual in this group because he appears in two separate service records.

The first records him as a coal miner from South Hetton, County Durham, attesting for the Royal Scots at Edinburgh in 1883, aged 18 years and six months. He served in South Africa and took part in the Bechuanaland Expedition of 1884-85.

Then the record takes a turn.

On 13 September 1889, Joseph deserted.

He did not rejoin until 16 November 1900, more than eleven years later. The papers therefore cover the period 1883 to 1900, but that does not mean seventeen years of ordinary continuous service.

That might have seemed the end of his military career. It was not.

Only a few months later he enlisted again, this time with the Scottish Horse / Imperial Yeomanry. His second service record shows him going to South Africa in 1901 and remaining there until August 1902. He was discharged at Aldershot on the termination of his engagement, with his character recorded simply as good.

Keeping the two records separate matters. They concern the same man, but they were created for different engagements and tell very different parts of his story.

Beyond the Army - Joseph Collingwood Wilson and the Royal Navy

Military records are not all Army records.

Joseph Collingwood Wilson, my second great-uncle, was born at Govan in 1854 and entered the Royal Navy as a boy aboard HMS Achilles in May 1871. His naval register traces him through a succession of ships and shore establishments, including Achilles, Royal Adelaide, Excellent, St Vincent, Agincourt, Canada, Valorous and Belleisle.

He later re-engaged and reached the rating of Able Seaman, but by 1887 he was invalided at Haslar. The surviving entry does not make the medical reason clear enough to state with confidence.

His record is a useful reminder that "military records" are not a single type of document. Different services created different administrative trails, and each has to be read on its own terms.

First World War fragments - Joseph Wilson and Salonica

First World War Army research brings another difficulty. Many soldiers' service records were destroyed or badly damaged during the Second World War, and the surviving WO 363 collection is known as the "Burnt Documents".

Some surviving files are substantial; others preserve only a few pages. Even a long file may contain blank forms, duplicates and administrative scraps alongside the few pages that really tell us something.

Joseph Wilson, my grandfather, was an 18-year-old coal miner when he enlisted in the Royal Scots at Edinburgh on 8 August 1914.

His surviving papers record service at home, then in France from March 1915, and later in Salonica. The key medical thread is malaria. Joseph stated that his attacks began on 12 June 1917 and attributed them to climatic conditions. His papers record treatment at Salonica, later illness, hospital movements and attachment to the Malaria Concentration Centre at Berwick-on-Tweed.

At his medical examination in February 1919, the disability was assessed at less than 20 per cent. He was demobilised the following month.

What does a surviving service file actually look like?

Robert Stirling Whitelaw, second cousin of my grandmother Caroline Mathieson Whitelaw, has a file containing 36 images.

That sounds like a complete military biography. In practice, many of those images are blank forms, duplicate sheets, printed instructions, covers or sparsely completed pages.

Hidden among them, however, are important details. Robert was an engineer who served as a gunner in the Royal Garrison Artillery, qualified as a first-class signaller, went to France and was treated for trench fever.

That is typical of the work involved in these records: preserving the whole surviving file while identifying the few pages that carry the personal story.

William Bowman Seton - when there is no ordinary service record

William Bowman Seton was a cousin of Val's great-grandmother Ada Orton, and his papers are different again.

Instead of a conventional soldier's service record, his evidence package consists mainly of the documents surrounding his education and nomination for Addiscombe Military College, together with a later Bombay Military Fund register.

William was born at Bombay on 1 September 1840 and baptised at St Thomas's Cathedral there in January 1841. His father, Bruce Seton, was already serving in the Bombay Army.

The surviving papers show William's education at Cheltenham College, his nomination for Addiscombe, examination answers, declarations, character certificates and medical certification. On 1 June 1860 he was formally certified as qualified for admission to the Royal Indian Military College.

The final document is from much later in his life: a Bombay Military Fund register recording contributions, retirement in March 1885 and death in August of the same year.

This is perhaps the clearest example of the wider point. Reconstructing military service is not always a matter of finding a document headed "Service Record". Education papers, nomination files, medical certificates, pension records and administrative registers may all provide pieces of the story.

Preserving the records

This work began as a housekeeping exercise.

The military documents had been collected over many years from several websites and archives. Some individuals had a few images; others had dozens. Some records were complete, some damaged, and some had no surviving conventional service file at all.

Each selected set has now been converted into a consolidated evidence package containing a synopsis, chronology and the surviving original images. The original individual files have also been retained separately.

A few of these packages will be made available through the family history website so that interested readers can see the complete documents rather than only the extracts reproduced in the newsletter.

William James Gilroy, my grandfather, also forms part of this collection. His service and the accident which cost him his right eye were covered in detail in the June newsletter, so I have not repeated that story here. His surviving military material has, however, now been brought into the same evidence-package format as the other records.

The next stage is less glamorous but equally important: reorganising the Military Records collection in TNG, linking the new PDFs to the appropriate people and sources, and preparing the material for later systematic remediation of the military source records.

What began as an exercise in tidying old downloads has produced something rather more valuable: a collection in which the records themselves can once again tell the stories.
