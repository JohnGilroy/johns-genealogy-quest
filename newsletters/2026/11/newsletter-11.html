From the Swing Riots to Van Diemen’s Land

The Alexander Brothers of Ramsbury

Two names appear next to one another in a Tasmanian convict appropriation list: Matthias Alexander, number 428, and Joseph Alexander, number 429. Beside their names are their ages, trades and assignments. Behind those few lines lies a family story stretching from Ramsbury in Wiltshire to Van Diemen’s Land.

The brothers had been convicted of destroying a threshing machine during the agricultural disturbances of 1830. They were transported together. Joseph’s wife Elizabeth and their three children stayed in England.

The records allow us to follow both sides of that separation: the brothers’ imprisonment, pardons and later lives in Tasmania, and Elizabeth’s work and family in Wiltshire.

The Swing Riots — and a Wiltshire family

Threshing separated grain from its stalks and husks. For agricultural labourers, doing it by hand provided work during autumn and winter. The introduction of threshing machines threatened that employment at a time of low wages and hardship.

In 1830, protests spread across parts of rural England. Machines were broken, threatening letters were sent and fires were set. The disturbances became known as the Swing Riots, after the fictitious “Captain Swing” whose name appeared on threatening letters.

That is the wider setting. For this family, the evidence is more specific: two brothers from Ramsbury, a conviction for machine destruction and sentences that took them overseas.

Joseph and Matthias were sons of Edward and Jane Alexander. Edward was Val’s four-times-great-grandfather, placing the brothers in her family story. Ramsbury’s parish register records Joseph’s baptism in March 1806 and Matthias’s in October 1810. These are baptism dates; they do not establish the exact dates on which the boys were born.

Joseph married Elizabeth Brown at Ramsbury on 30 November 1826. Her surname also appears as Browne in the family research. The parish registers then name Joseph and Elizabeth as the parents of Josiah, Elisha and Mary Anne, baptised in 1827, 1828 and 1830. In those entries Joseph is described as a labourer.

Before the convict records begin, therefore, we already have a local family: parents, children and work in Ramsbury. Transportation would divide it.

A machine destroyed

The Home Office criminal register lists the brothers under the Wiltshire Special Commission of 1830–1831. The offence is given as “feloniously destroying a threshing machine”, and the sentence against each name is seven years’ transportation.

The prison-hulk register supplies a more precise conviction date: 27 December 1830, at New Sarum. Their Tasmanian conduct records also carry 27 December 1830. This is the recorded conviction date, rather than a date for the destruction of the machine.

The distinction matters. These documents establish the offence for which the brothers were convicted and the punishment imposed. They do not, by themselves, provide a full account of the incident or tell us why either man took part.

For Joseph, the sentence also meant separation from a wife and three young children. The children’s baptisms had all taken place before his conviction.

From the Hardy to the Eliza

On 21 January 1831, the brothers were received aboard the prison hulk Hardy, moored at Portsmouth. They are adjacent entries there too: Mathias Alexander, number 1300, and Joseph Alexander, number 1301. The spelling of Matthias’s name varies between records.

The hulk register records machine breaking and a seven-year sentence for both men. It gives Joseph’s age as 25 and Matthias’s as 18. Later Tasmanian lists give Matthias’s age as 20; those recorded ages should be kept as they stand.

From the Hardy, their documented journey continues aboard Eliza, which brought them to Van Diemen’s Land in May 1831. The colony was later known as Tasmania.

The appropriation list records Matthias, aged 20, as a hurdle maker, and Joseph, aged 25, as a carpenter and wheelwright. Matthias’s assignment is written “V.D.L. Establishmt.”; Joseph’s is written “TW”. The latter abbreviation has not been securely expanded.

Recorded by the convict authorities

A separate description register turns those names and trades into individual descriptions. Matthias is recorded with brown hair and blue eyes, and described as “Stout made”.

Joseph’s entry gives brown hair and grey eyes. His face is described as round and slightly pock-pitted. The clerk also noted a mole on his left arm and a scar on the back of his left hand.

These details were recorded by the authorities to identify the men. For a family historian, they offer a different kind of evidence from the parish registers: occupations, appearance and distinguishing marks.

Joseph’s conduct record adds a detail about the life he had left behind. It describes him as married and his wife as a shopkeeper at Ramsbury, Wiltshire. That is the description in this record; it need not mean that she followed the same occupation throughout her later life.

The family left behind

Elizabeth’s story continues in English records. In the 1841 census, Elizabeth Alexander appears at Blind Lane, Ramsbury, immediately followed by Josiah, aged 13, Elisha, aged 12, and Mary, aged 10.

The census does not state their relationships. The earlier baptisms provide the evidence that Josiah, Elisha and Mary Anne were Joseph and Elizabeth’s children. Taken together, the records place the family left behind in Ramsbury a decade after transportation.

Elizabeth’s occupation in 1841 is written “Ag. Lab.”, the abbreviation for agricultural labourer. By 1851, she was enumerated in a household at Froxfield, working as a nurse. That return describes her as married and gives Ramsbury as her birthplace.

Those entries show that Elizabeth worked, and that she was still recorded as married twenty years after Joseph’s departure. They do not tell us what contact she had with him or what she knew about his life overseas.

Her burial is recorded at Ramsbury on 11 December 1855, aged 50, with Ramsbury given as her abode. The burial entry does not supply an exact date of death.

It would be easy to let the convict records dominate this story. Elizabeth’s census and burial entries bring it back to the family in England: the children growing older, her recorded work, and a life that continued in Wiltshire.

Pardons and new beginnings

Both brothers’ conduct records contain the same significant date: 3 February 1836. Matthias received Free Pardon number 298; Joseph received Free Pardon number 313.

The later civil registers show them in different circumstances. In January 1854, Matthias married Ann Eden Thewlis at Launceston. The register describes him as a farmer and widower. His 1865 death record gives his occupation as licensed victualler — a very different entry from the hurdle maker listed on arrival.

Joseph also appears as a farmer when he married Mary Dyer on 26 May 1854 at the Deputy Registrar’s Office, Horton. Elizabeth was still alive in England. His marital condition in this marriage entry has not been securely transcribed, so the record cannot yet support an explanation of how he presented his circumstances.

On 12 January 1856, he married Harriet Gee at the Table Cape Inn. This time the register explicitly describes him as a widower. Elizabeth’s burial had taken place the previous month.

The dates can be set beside one another. They do not tell us what Joseph or Elizabeth knew, or explain Joseph’s decisions. The story is strongest when that limit remains visible.

Joseph died in 1878. His death register again describes him as a farmer. The records now span almost half a century, from a Wiltshire machine-breaking conviction to the later lives of two pardoned men in Tasmania.

Another Alexander transported

A related case concerns John Alexander, transported for sending a threatening letter. His conduct record gives a Wiltshire trial on 5 March 1831, a life sentence and arrival aboard Lord William Bentinck in August 1832.

His identification as the Ramsbury-born brother remains probable, rather than certain. The family baptism is dated December 1812; the convict description gives an age of 28 in 1832; and a Ramsbury-born John Alexander’s death entry gives an age of 79 in 1898. These conflicting ages remain unresolved.

A family divided by the world

The surviving portrait carries a museum identification. Beside it, the written records trace Joseph from Ramsbury, through the Hardy and Eliza, to farming in Tasmania. Matthias’s records follow the same early route before continuing through marriage and his later occupation.

For the next generation, the clearest starting point remains the three children whose baptisms were recorded in Ramsbury. Their father’s transportation did not take the whole household overseas. Elizabeth and the children belong at the centre of the story alongside the transported brothers.

We can document the convictions, voyage, pardons, work, marriages and deaths. We cannot recover every conversation or fill every silence between England and Tasmania.

What emerges is a family history with two continuing settings: Ramsbury and Wiltshire for Elizabeth and the children; Tasmania for Joseph and Matthias. A few adjacent names in a register open onto a much larger story — one that reaches across the world without losing sight of the people who stayed behind.
